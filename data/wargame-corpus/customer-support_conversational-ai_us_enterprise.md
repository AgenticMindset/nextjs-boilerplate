## Key Players

- Sierra — Bret Taylor/Clay Bavor's agentic AI company for Fortune 1000 consumer brands, sold on pure outcome-based pricing and white-glove implementation.
- Intercom Fin — Incumbent help-desk vendor's AI agent, reasons across the full knowledge base and takes actions in connected systems, priced per resolution.
- Ada — No-code/low-code AI agent builder, originally mid-market-friendly but has pushed upmarket into enterprise CX teams.
- Decagon — Newer well-funded entrant focused on high-volume enterprise support automation with heavy vertical customization (fintech, marketplaces, travel).
- Kore.ai / NiCE Cognigy (post-acquisition) — Legacy enterprise contact-center IVA vendors selling into large, security-conscious CX/telephony estates.

## Positioning

The US enterprise segment has split into two camps: "outcome-based" agentic entrants (Sierra, Decagon, Fin) that price on resolutions rather than seats, and legacy contact-center platforms (NiCE/Cognigy, Kore.ai, Genesys) that bundle AI agents into existing IVR/telephony contracts. Sierra and Decagon target logo-name consumer brands and win on speed-to-value and reasoning quality; Fin leverages Intercom's existing installed base to upsell AI on top of the help desk. Legacy players compete on compliance, omnichannel breadth (voice + chat + email) and existing procurement relationships rather than model quality.

## Pricing Model

Enterprise deals are overwhelmingly usage/outcome-based rather than per-seat: Fin charges roughly $0.99 per resolution with no platform fee, while Sierra and Decagon negotiate custom outcome-based contracts (price per resolved conversation or per outcome) that scale into the mid six-to-seven-figure annual range for large brands. Legacy contact-center incumbents (Cognigy/NiCE, Kore.ai, Genesys) still favor annual platform licenses layered with per-seat agent-assist and per-minute/per-session voice fees, typically requiring multi-year enterprise agreements and professional-services attach.

## Known Weaknesses

Legacy contact-center platforms carry significant IVR-era technical debt, slow release cycles, and reputations for brittle, rules-based "chatbot" experiences that enterprises are actively trying to replace — creating an active displacement wave that outcome-based entrants are riding. The newer agentic vendors, conversely, are unproven at extreme scale/regulatory complexity (healthcare, banking) and their outcome-based pricing can become unpredictable and hard to budget for CFOs used to flat SaaS spend. Nearly all vendors still struggle with deep, multi-system "agentic" actions (refunds, account changes) that require reliable write-access integrations, which remains a common enterprise deployment blocker.

## Distribution Channels

Enterprise deals in the US are almost entirely direct-sales-led with named-account teams, executive sponsorship, and lengthy security/compliance review (SOC 2, HIPAA, data-residency) before procurement. System integrators and CX consultancies (Accenture, Deloitte, Genesys/NICE partner ecosystems) heavily influence RFPs for legacy incumbents; newer entrants like Sierra and Decagon win largely through founder-led sales, board/investor introductions, and case-study-driven inbound from CX and CFO buyers rather than channel partners.

## Recent Moves

- Sierra scaled to roughly $100M ARR within about 21 months of launch, reinforcing outcome-based pricing as a credible enterprise model rather than a startup gimmick.
- Decagon and other agentic entrants have continued raising large growth rounds through 2025-2026 to fund vertical-specific enterprise deployments (fintech, travel, marketplaces).
- NiCE's acquisition of Cognigy consolidated a Europe-born enterprise IVA platform into a major US contact-center incumbent, intensifying competition for large omnichannel deals.
- Intercom has continued to push Fin's per-resolution pricing and expand its autonomous action set (refunds, order tracking, multi-step workflows) to compete directly with outcome-based pure-plays.
